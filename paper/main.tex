\title{From Packing to Routing: Cross-Layer Design Space Exploration for IMC
DNN Accelerators on 3D NoCs}

\begin{abstract}
In-memory computing removes weight traffic from DNN accelerators but relocates
the rest onto the on-chip network, where hundreds of crossbar tiles reduce
partial sums through a few accumulator ports. Communication is then set by
three decisions --- how crossbars are packed into tiles, where tiles are placed,
and how packets are routed --- each optimised today with the others held fixed.
This paper explores the three as one design space on a 3D NoC, in
cycle-accurate simulation of three DNN blocks. The layers couple through one
quantity, the port floor that packing fixes: choosing packing for that floor
rather than for traffic volume lowers mean delay $11.6\times$ and p99
$18.6\times$; below the floor, a zero-cost placement refinement recovers up to
$46\%$ of delay and $67\%$ of p99; and adaptive selection wins in both regimes
($1.85\times$/$2.39\times$ above, $1.15\times$/$1.23\times$ below) yet leaves
a residue below the floor that no offline metric predicts. Packing thus sets
the operating point that placement can only reach and routing can only defend,
while the residue routing leaves is temporal, calling for selection that adapts
in time as well as space.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presents a cross-layer design flow for IMC DNN accelerators on a
3D NoC. Prior studies design for a single layer, which limits design freedom
and forgoes the gains shown here. The proposed flow is evaluated on three
workloads. The design layers are found to be highly coupled. The first layer
(packing), which decides the port floor, is shown to have the highest impact:
the optimal design choice for delay lowers mean delay $11.6\times$ and p99
$18.7\times$ at matched load. Below that floor, mapping can be optimized
through a proposed metric (escape slope); around the knee, at no communication
cost, this metric is shown to recover up to $46\%$ of mean delay and $67\%$ of
p99. At the selection layer, adaptive selection is the better fixed policy in
both regimes, by $1.85\times$/$2.39\times$ above the floor and
$1.15\times$/$1.23\times$ below it.

Always-DP is shown to capture $96$--$98\%$ of the improvement an
oracle switching policy per placement and load would achieve above the floor and $71$--$78\%$ below it. No offline
quantity predicts which cells it loses, because the residue is temporal:
operating below the floor produces transient congestion clustered at phase
boundaries, on a timescale a spatially adaptive policy cannot track. Closing
this residue motivates a temporally as well as spatially adaptive selection
policy. The same design flow admits other objectives, such as thermal-aware
placement and load balancing.
