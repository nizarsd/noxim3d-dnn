\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Experimental Tools and Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
To evaluate the  proposed design flow and metrics, we employ Noxim, a cycle-accurate SystemC-based NoC simulator \cite{cataniaCycleAccurateNetworkChip2016}.  Noxim is a 2D NoC simulator, so we extended it to enable 3D and implemented the routing and selection used in this work which were not available in the original version: namely the balanced odd-even routing \cite{dahirHighlyAdaptiveDeadlockFree2013} and dynamic programming network selection \cite{makDPnetworkOptimalDynamic2009}. 

The DP cost field reconvergence time is determined by the topology diameter and node count. For the target mesh it is 648 cycles with DP clocked at $4\times$ the network clock.

The 3D NoC size is a $6\times6\times3$ mesh of 108 tiles. Each tile holds $c$ $128\times128$ crossbars of single-bit cells, with 8-bit weights spread over eight bit-planes. Flit size is 4 bytes, the packet is fixed at 16 flits, input buffers size is 16 flits, and the substrate runs at 1GHz clock. NoC links and tile's injection/ejection ports carry one 4-byte flit per 1 ns cycle, giving 4 GB/s per channel, with independent injection and ejection channels. 

Changing the mesh simultaneously redefines both the mapping space and the routing substrate, because topology is not another layer below routing but a parallel design axis. Thus, in this work the topology is fixed to keep the layer attribution identifiable.
\subsection{Workloads and Traffic Generation}
\label{sec:workloads}
Workloads are ResNet-50 \cite{heDeepResidualLearning2016}, VGG-16 \cite{simonyanVeryDeepConvolutional2014}  and DeiT-S \cite{touvronTrainingDataefficientImage2020}. The three DNN workloads span a residual CNN, a plain CNN and a transformer encoder. They differ in partial-sum-to-activation ratio from $8\times$ (DeiT-S) to $33\times$ (VGG-16), and NoC bandwidth demand, by $5$–$6\times$, and in structure: a deep reduction funnel with a parallel shortcut (ResNet-50), load distributed across many sinks of similar weight with no isolated funnel (DeiT-S), and a sequence of deep reductions (VGG-16). They are converted offline into statistical traffic tables the simulator can run, with per-pair injection rates and phase windows. Table~\ref{tab:workloads} lists the three workloads. A workload inference period (compute time) is identical across its packings, and the activation and partial-sum volumes shown are per inference and packing-independent. 

 All runs use an appropriate warm-up and are averaged over 3 to 30 seeds, with offered load swept by a scalar multiplier $k$ on all injection rates. Unless stated otherwise, comparisons are made inside the knee window, defined as the load range in which mean BL delay stays below an empirically set multiple ($30\times$) of its free-flow value. Because mappings differ in their saturation loads, capacity is measured per mapping as $\kstar$: the injection scale at which a mapping's delay reaches twice its \emph{own} free-flow delay. The mapping search and the computation of design-time metrics (PF, PL, CC, ES)  are  done in Python from the traffic tables. Approximately 36 000 simulation runs were conducted to obtain the results below.

\subsection{Design Flow and Search Procedure}
\label{sec:search}
The flow evaluated below selects the orientation that minimizes $\PF$, places
at minimum communication cost, refines by maximizing $\mathrm{ES}$ subject to
$\mathrm{CC}\le\mathrm{CC}_{\min}$ and $\PL\le\PL(\mathrm{CC}_{\min})$,
minimizes $\PL$ where search budget remains, and applies adaptive selection;
each step is evaluated with the layers above it at their chosen point.
Placements are searched by a guarded hill-climb from a random permutation,
300 steps of 50 candidate moves (a transposition, or with probability $0.3$ a
relocation to an idle node), the constraint applied in the move filter and the
best strictly improving move accepted. All searches are seeded and
reproducible.

\subsection{Packing Impact}
\label{sec:r1}

The packing layer acts before any placement exists, so we first ask what it
alone decides. For each workload we enumerate every feasible $(c,r,s)$ --- 34
points in all --- and evaluate $\PF$ offline from the traffic graph. Within
each density $c$ we identify the orientation minimising total NoC traffic, the
classical pre-network objective, and the one minimising $\PF$.

Table~\ref{tab:packing} reports both. The two objectives coincide in four of
the nine $(\text{workload}, c)$ cells and select different orientations in the
remaining five. Where they differ, ranking on $\PF$ lowers the floor by
$1.27$--$3.15\times$ at a cost of $1.07$--$1.67\times$ in traffic; since the
inference period is fixed per workload, that is also the ratio of average NoC
bandwidth. Across ResNet-50's eleven feasible points $\PF$ spans
$0.258$--$1.494$ flits/cycle, a factor of $5.8$. Orientation therefore fixes
the floor, and no later decision can lower it.

Whether a lower floor is worth its traffic overhead cannot be settled offline,
so we simulate the widest disagreement on ResNet-50: $c{=}16$, the min-$\PF$
orientation $(2,8)$ against the min-traffic orientation $(4,4)$, a
$2.78\times$ lower floor for $8\%$ more traffic. To keep the comparison at the
packing layer, both arms receive minimum-communication-cost placements and are
swept over the same load ladder, three placements per arm at three seeds per
rung, under local selection (BL).

Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} shows both arms across the ladder: the min-traffic arm
bends between $k = 0.7$ and $1.0$ and is saturated by $1.6$, while the
min-$\PF$ arm bends at $2.0$--$2.5$, a $1.85\times$ higher $\kstar$. At
$k = 1.0$ the gap is $11.6\times$ in mean delay and $18.6\times$ in p99, and
it widens with load. Both arms stay below their own floor throughout
($\PL/\PF \le 0.58$), so the gap is not a placement artefact, and under DP the
same rung reads $13.8\times$ / $20.3\times$.

minimum-traffic objective is the wrong one to rank orientations by: packing
sets the operating point that every downstream layer inherits.

\subsection{Mapping Exploration}
\label{sec:r2}

With the packing fixed, the mapping layer controls one quantity, $\PL$, and
we ask whether moving it moves delay. The population is ResNet-50 at its
$c{=}8$ minimum-$\PF$ packing $(2,4)$, $\PF = 0.392$: 24 placements produced
by the guarded search, spanning $\PL = 0.127$--$0.490$ ($3.9\times$) with path
variety pinned so that $\PL$ is the only predictor that varies, seven of them
above the floor. Each placement is simulated at two loads, $k = 1.60$ and
$1.80$, with 30 seeds each, under local selection (BL).

Fig.~\ref{fig:hinge} plots mean delay and p99 against $\PL$ at $k = 1.80$.
Delay does not respond to $\PL$ until $\PL$ crosses $\PF$ and responds
sharply afterwards: a regression with a hinge at the floor reaches $R^2 =
0.735$ against $0.404$ for a straight line, with a below-floor slope
indistinguishable from zero ($t = -0.16$) and an above-floor slope of $+655$
cycles per unit load ($t = +5.13$, $p < 10^{-4}$). The pattern replicates at
$k = 1.60$ ($R^2 = 0.705$), with the above-floor slope $64$--$72\times$ the
below-floor one at both loads. Delay therefore tracks $\BIND = \max(\PF,\PL)$ ($r = +0.86$), and which of its
two terms is active is decided by whether the placement has crossed the
floor.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{hill_pl_hinge_paper.pdf}
  \caption{Mean delay vs.\ $\PL$, 24 placements of ResNet-50 $(8,2,4)$ at
  $k = 1.80$ under BL, hinge fit at $\PF = 0.392$.}
  \label{fig:hinge}
\end{figure}

Hop count acts separately but weakly: on three arms of the same packing at
identical $\BIND$ below the floor, $15\%$ more communication cost adds
$3.7$--$6.7\%$ to free-flow delay, while adding $\mathrm{CC}$ to a $\BIND$
model moves its adjusted $R^2$ by at most $0.01$ --- communication cost sets
where the delay curve starts, $\BIND$ where it bends.

Below the floor placement still matters in a band around the knee --- eight
placements of ResNet-50 $(8,1,8)$ spread $1.34\times$, $13.62\times$ and
$1.28\times$ in mean delay at light load, the knee and saturation --- but
searching past minimum-communication-cost placements returns at best
$1.14\times$ ($1.34\times$ in p99) on about six pairs, so minimum
communication cost already captures nearly all of it.

Above the floor the picture inverts. Across five above-floor populations on
the three workloads, $n = 8$ to $24$ placements each at three seeds and read
in the knee window, delay spreads $4.6$--$31.8\times$ between the best and
worst placement, and $\PL/\PF$ orders that spread with a positive correlation
in every population. Above the floor, placement determines delay and $\PL$ is
what to search on.

Finally, five causes that could push a placement across the floor without
intent (min-CC mapping, thermal spreading, link faults, TSV clustering,
multi-tenancy) all leave $\PL/\PF \le 1.000$ when screened offline: the
above-floor regime is entered by provisioning, not by accident.

The mapping layer's task below the floor is therefore to reach minimum
communication cost and stay clear of the knee band. What a placement can still
do for free once it is there is the subject of the next section.

\subsection{Mapping Refinement}
\label{sec:refine}

Below the floor the previous section leaves the mapping layer at minimum
communication cost, and we ask whether anything is still free once
communication cost and $\PL$ are held. Escapable load $\Esc$ does not predict
capacity ($\kstar$ correlates with $\PL$ at $-0.54$ to $-0.84$ in five
populations while $\Esc$'s sign varies), so escape room is tested for its
effect on delay by intervention, since it co-varies with $\PL$ and
communication cost in any sampled population. We therefore fix the packing and
the seed, start from the minimum-communication-cost placement, and run two
guarded climbs from it: one minimising and one maximising the escape slope
$\mathrm{ES} = \Esc_{20} - \Esc_{5}$, both constrained to
$\mathrm{CC} \le \mathrm{CC}_{\min}$ and $\PL \le \PL(\mathrm{CC}_{\min})$.
The two arms then differ only in how escape room is distributed across the
congested links. This is done on DeiT-S $(16,1,16)$, VGG-16 $(8,4,2)$ and
ResNet-50 $(8,2,4)$, eight seeds each, under both selection policies, read at
each arm's knee rung.

Table~\ref{tab:es} reports the paired result. Maximising $\mathrm{ES}$ lowers
mean delay by $1.13$--$1.25\times$ and p99 by $1.26$--$1.43\times$ at the arm
level, and by up to $1.85\times$ and $2.99\times$ on the best individual
placement --- $46\%$ of mean delay and $67\%$ of p99 --- at no cost in
communication or peak link load. Combining the three arms gives
$p = 0.025$ on mean delay and $p = 0.016$ on p99 under BL, and $p = 0.046$ on
mean delay under DP. The effect is confined to the knee (it is gone one rung
past it) and it is not a property of the selection unit: which policy collects
it varies by arm.

The lever has a boundary that is visible in advance: on VGG-16 $(32,4,8)$,
where one pair carries $92\%$ of the peak link, the intervention returns
nothing, and whether a guarded climb can move $\mathrm{ES}$ at all --- an
offline check costing minutes --- called the outcome in all four cases, with
all nine minimum-$\PF$ orientations on the responding side. Escape shape is
therefore a zero-cost design objective for the mapping layer; what remains is
decided at run time.

\subsection{Routing and Selection}
\label{sec:r3}

The last layer acts at run time. With packing and placement fixed, selection
decides which of the admissible outputs a packet takes, and we compare the
local buffer-level policy (BL) used so far against the distributed cost-to-go
policy (DP) on identical placements and seeds, so that the policy is the only
difference between paired runs.

On 71 above-floor placements from the populations of Sec.~\ref{sec:r2}, read
in the knee window, DP is faster than BL on 65, by $1.15\times$ in mean delay
and $1.36\times$ in p99 on average and up to $1.64\times$ and $2.35\times$ on
a single placement. Below the floor the same test gives a smaller gain that
peaks at each packing's own knee ($1.10$--$1.35\times$ mean across three
sets). The regimes differ in magnitude, not in sign.

DP's second effect is on the \emph{spread} of delay across placements, which
Sec.~\ref{sec:r2} showed to be the mapping layer's main risk. We take every
population in which BL exhibits such a spread --- eight in all, spanning the
three workloads, three densities, eight orientations and both regimes,
$n = 7$ to $24$ placements at three seeds --- and read the ratio of the
slowest to the fastest placement under each policy inside that population's
knee window. Fig.~\ref{fig:spread} shows one population in each regime.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{f5_two_regime.pdf}
  \caption{Delay across placements, BL vs.\ DP: 12 equal-$\PL$ placements of
  ResNet-50 $(16,2,8)$ above the floor (top) and 8 placements of $(8,1,8)$
  at the knee below it (bottom).}
  \label{fig:spread}
\end{figure}

DP narrows the spread in all eight populations, by $1.09\times$ to
$7.50\times$. In Fig.~\ref{fig:spread} the equal-$\PL$ population above the
floor moves from $64.3\times$ to $8.6\times$ in mean delay, and the knee
population below it from $13.6\times$ to $7.6\times$ ($22.6\times$ to
$14.0\times$ in p99). The mechanism is
rescue of the worst placements rather than acceleration of the best: on the
fastest placement of each population BL matches or beats DP, while on the
slowest DP improves delay by up to $7.6\times$ ($5\,566$ to $732$ cycles).
What DP cannot compress is variation that $\PL$ itself causes, since that is
$\BIND$: where the spread comes from a $\PL$ range the ratio is unchanged.

The compression window is the knee window of Sec.~\ref{sec:r2}: adaptive
selection is a throughput lever above the floor and a robustness lever
wherever placements differ, and both live at the knee.

\subsection{Policy Choice and Its Limit}
\label{sec:r4}

We ask which fixed policy is better over everything measured, and how much a
policy that could switch per operating point would add. Every knee-window
placement $\times$ load cell from the populations above is used --- $701$
above the floor, $128$ below, three seeds each --- and three policies are
compared on identical cells: always-BL, always-DP, and a per-cell oracle
taking the better of the two.

Fig.~\ref{fig:policy} shows the result. DP is ahead of BL in both regimes and
at every load band: by $1.85\times$ in mean delay and $2.39\times$ in p99 above
the floor, and by $1.15\times$ and $1.23\times$ below it. The oracle adds
$1.1\%$ and $2.5\%$ above the floor but $3.8\%$ and $7.7\%$ below it: of the
improvement available between always-BL and the oracle, always-DP captures
$98\%$ and $96\%$ above the floor but only $78\%$ and $71\%$ below it.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{f6_policy.pdf}
  \caption{Left: DP's advantage over BL per cell vs.\ congestion. Right:
  speed-up over always-BL of always-DP and of a per-cell oracle, by regime.}
  \label{fig:policy}
\end{figure}

The remaining $4$--$8\%$ below the floor is the only headroom left, so we ask
whether the cells DP loses can be identified at design time. About thirty
offline quantities correlate with DP's per-cell gain at $|r| \le 0.4$, none
surviving a held-out packing, and a pre-registered learned model over $15$
offline features of $56$ placements from seven packings reaches
$R^2 = -0.03$ on DP's delay and p99 gain when a packing is held out, against
a registered ceiling of $0.10$.

Adaptive selection is therefore the right fixed policy in both regimes, and
what it leaves below the floor is not a design-time quantity.

\label{sec:r5}
\emph{Scope.} $\PL$, $\PL_f$ and $\Esc$ are offline path-enumeration models under an
even-spreading assumption that BL, in particular, does not meet. $\PL$ has
been checked against the simulator on one placement (seven links spanning
ranks 1--50: measured/model $0.88$--$1.01$ under BL, rank order
$\rho = +0.89$); per-flow path counts have not, and none is quoted. All
results use one $6{\times}6{\times}3$ mesh and one buffer depth, neither
swept. Partial sums are carried at INT32, which overstates the reduction
traffic the flow exploits; placement rankings survive at 16-bit on ResNet-50
and VGG-16. Spread and best-case ratios are max/min statistics over
$7$--$24$ placements at three seeds.
