\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Experimental Tools and Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
To evaluate the  proposed design flow and metrics, we employ Noxim, a cycle-accurate SystemC-based NoC simulator \cite{cataniaCycleAccurateNetworkChip2016}.  Noxim is a 2D NoC simulator, so we extended it to enable 3D and implemented the routing and selection used in this work which were not available in the original version: namely the balanced odd-even routing \cite{dahirHighlyAdaptiveDeadlockFree2013} and dynamic programming network selection \cite{makDPnetworkOptimalDynamic2009}. 

The DP cost field reconvergence time is determined by the topology diameter and node count. For the target mesh it is 648 cycles with DP clocked at $4\times$ the network clock.

A 3D mesh NoC is adopted building on recent work in tiled DNN accelerators~\cite{liTTNNMThermalTrafficAware2024,narangTEFLONThermallyEfficient2024}. The main motive for 3D NoC is the rise in fan-out and fan-in, which the
third dimension provides (4 links per node in 2D vs 6 in 3D) and which the funnel DNN bottleneck can best exploit. This is also confirmed by our results: confining a 36-node placement to one
plane of the same mesh, against a $3\times4\times3$ block (packing $(32,4,8)$,
three workloads, three seeds), costs up to $40\%$ in communication cost and
$50\%$ in peak link load, and $2.2\times$ in p99 under congestion. Load
capacity, however, is unchanged, since it is packing-dependent and
placement-invariant and is set by the port floor in both geometries.


The NoC is a $6\times6\times3$ mesh of 108 tiles. A tile holds $c$ crossbars of $128\times128$   1-bit cells, with 8-bit weights spread over eight bit-planes; activations are INT8 and partial sums
INT32, as the accumulation depth requires. 

NoC Flit size is 4 bytes, the packet is fixed at 16 flits, input buffers size is 16 flits, and the substrate runs at 1GHz clock. NoC links and tile's injection/ejection ports carry one 4-byte flit per 1 ns cycle, giving 4 GB/s per channel, with independent injection and ejection channels. 

Changing the mesh simultaneously redefines both the mapping space and the routing substrate, because topology is not another layer below routing but a parallel design axis. Thus, in this work the topology is fixed to keep the layer attribution identifiable.
\subsection{Workloads and Traffic Generation}
\label{sec:workloads}
Workloads are ResNet-50 \cite{heDeepResidualLearning2016}, VGG-16 \cite{simonyanVeryDeepConvolutional2014}  and DeiT-S \cite{touvronTrainingDataefficientImage2020}. The three DNN workloads span a residual CNN, a plain CNN and a transformer encoder. They differ in partial-sum-to-activation ratio from $8\times$ (DeiT-S) to $33\times$ (VGG-16), and NoC bandwidth demand, by $5$–$6\times$, and in structure: a deep reduction funnel with a parallel shortcut (ResNet-50), load distributed across many sinks of similar weight with no isolated funnel (DeiT-S), and a sequence of deep reductions (VGG-16). They are converted offline into statistical traffic tables the simulator can run, with per-pair injection rates and phase windows. Table~\ref{tab:workloads} lists the three workloads. A workload inference period (compute time) is identical across its packings, and the activation and partial-sum volumes shown are per inference and packing-independent. 

 All runs use an appropriate warm-up and are averaged over 3 to 30 seeds, with offered load swept by a scalar multiplier $k$ on all injection rates, where $k=1$ is a per-workload reference scale rather than a nominal operating point. Unless stated otherwise, comparisons are made inside the knee window, defined as the load range in which mean BL delay stays below an empirically set multiple ($30\times$) of its low-load delay, the delay at the lightest load level simulated. Because mappings differ in their saturation loads, capacity is measured per mapping as $\kstar$: the injection scale at which a mapping's delay reaches twice its \emph{own} low-load delay. The mapping search and the computation of design-time metrics (PF, PL, CC, ES)  are  done in Python from the traffic tables. Approximately 36 600 simulation runs were conducted to obtain the results below.

\subsection{Design Flow and Search Procedure}
\label{sec:search}
To evaluate the proposed design flow, the orientation that minimizes $\PF$ at a
given packing density $c$ is selected and placed with minimum communication
cost ($\mathrm{CC}$) mappings. The placement is refined
by maximizing $\mathrm{ES}$ subject to $\mathrm{CC}\le\mathrm{CC}_{\min}$ and
$\PL\le\PL(\mathrm{CC}_{\min})$, and adaptive selection is applied; $\PL$ is
normally below the floor but the mapping and selection experiments deliberately
cross it to expose that regime. Mappings are found by a seeded guarded hill-climb with 300 steps of 50 candidates each. Moves violating the constraints are rejected.
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{f_po_ladder_norm.pdf}
  \caption{Min-$\PF$ (dotted) vs.\ min-traffic (dashed) orientation under
  BL, to $3\times$ the min-traffic knee; load relative to that arm's $\kstar$,
  delay relative to zero-load delay; bars span three placements, ticks mark
  the min-$\PF$ arm's $\kstar$.}
  \label{fig:ladder}
\end{figure}

The baseline at each layer is the objective previous work optimizes: minimum
traffic volume for packing, minimum communication cost $\mathrm{CC}$ for mapping~\cite{liTTNNMThermalTrafficAware2024}, and local
buffer-level selection for routing. Each layer is then evaluated against its baseline
with the upper layers at their best.

\subsection{Packing Impact}
\label{sec:r1}
The packing layer decides $(c,r,s)$ for a given workload. It acts before any placement exists and determines, $\PF$, the number of tiles required, and the total inter-tile bandwidth. All can be computed offline from the traffic graph. Three density values are evaluated; 8, 16, and 32 CBs/tile. Within each density $c$ we identify the orientation, $(r,s)$, minimizing total NoC
traffic, the classical pre-network objective, and the one minimizing $\PF$. Table~\ref{tab:packing} reports both. The two objectives coincide in four of
the nine $(\text{workload}, c)$ cells. In the
remaining five, ranking on $\PF$ lowers the floor by
$1.27$--$3.15\times$ at a cost of $1.07$--$1.67\times$ in traffic.

To determine whether a lower floor justifies its traffic overhead,
so we simulate each workload's largest floor-ratio pair (min-$\PF$ and min-traffic) from
Table~\ref{tab:packing}. To exclude the impact of mapping  and keep the 
comparison at the packing layer, both arms receive
min $\mathrm{CC}$ placements and run under the same load
levels ($k$) with three placements per arm and three
seeds per level, under buffer-level
selection (BL).

Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} plots the three sweeps mean delay and p99 (relative to the measured zero-load delay) against load (relative to the
min-traffic arm's $\kstar$). It can be seen that all three min-traffic arms fall on one band and the three min-$\PF$ arms on a lower one. In terms of capacity, $\kstar$ is $1.75$--$2.10\times$ higher on the min-$\PF$
arm, and two load levels past the min-traffic knee. In terms of delay gain, the gap is $12\times$, $25\times$
and $64\times$ in mean delay and $19\times$, $30\times$ and $107\times$ in
p99. All mappings stay under their floor ($\PL/\PF \le 0.91$), so the gap is
not a placement artifact. The same holds under DP.

The min-$\PF$ traffic overhead is therefore justified on all three workloads and \emph{packing sets the operating point that every downstream layer inherits}.

\subsection{Mapping Exploration}
\label{sec:r2}
With $\PF$ fixed by packing, the mapping layer controls $\PL$, and we ask whether moving it moves delay. For ResNet-50 at its $c{=}8$ minimum-$\PF$ packing $(2,4)$, $\PF = 0.392$, 24 placements are generated using the hill-climb, spanning $\PL = 0.127$--$0.490$ ($3.9\times$), seven of them above the floor, i.e.\ link-bound. Each placement is simulated at $k =1.80$, with 30 seeds each, under local selection (BL).

Fig.~\ref{fig:hinge} plots mean delay against $\PL$ for those placements.
Delay shows no significant response to $\PL$ until $\PL$ crosses $\PF$ and responds
sharply afterward. This is confirmed by a regression with a hinge at the floor, which reaches $R^2 =
0.735$ against $0.404$ for a straight line: the below-floor slope is
near zero and the above-floor slope is $+655$ cycles per unit $\PL$. These results show that delay tracks $\BIND = \max(\PF,\PL)$, and which of its two terms is active depends on whether the placement has crossed the floor.

  $k = 1.80$ under BL, hinge fit at $\PF = 0.392$.}

Hop count acts separately but weakly. For the same  $\BIND$ below the floor,
$15\%$ more communication cost causes only a $3.7$--$6.7\%$ increase in low-load delay.

Placement below the floor still significantly impacts delay near the knee
($13.6\times$ across eight placements of ResNet-50 $(8,1,8)$ (see Fig.~\ref{fig:spread}), against
$1.3\times$ at light load or saturation). The min $\mathrm{CC}$ placement, however, is shown to capture most of it. Searching beyond min $\mathrm{CC}$ returns at most
$1.14\times$. The picture flips above the floor. Across five populations on
the three workloads ($n = 8$--$24$ each), delay spreads widely ($4.6$--$31.8\times$) between
best and worst placement. That spread is  ordered by $\PL/\PF$  in every population. The VGG-16 and DeiT-S workloads show the same trend: delay is nearly flat in $\PL$ below the floor and rises with it above.

\subsection{Mapping Refinement}
\label{sec:refine}
For the below-floor mapping at its min-$\mathrm{CC}$, we investigate whether there is a room for further refinement through maximizing the proposed metric $\mathrm{ES}$ with $\mathrm{CC}$ and $\PL$ held constant. Escape room varies with $\PL$ and communication cost, so we tested it by 
intervention. The packing and the seed are fixed and search start from the
min-$\mathrm{CC}$ placement, and run two guarded climbs from it, one
minimizing and one maximizing $\mathrm{ES} = \Esc_{20} - \Esc_{5}$, both constrained to
$\mathrm{CC} \le \mathrm{CC}_{\min}$ and $\PL \le \PL(\mathrm{CC}_{\min})$. The $5{\to}20$ span is the best of 18
endpoint pairs screened against measured gain on nine packings.
This is done on three arms, eight seeds each (Fig.~\ref{fig:es}).

Fig.~\ref{fig:es} shows the result. It can be seen that maximizing $\mathrm{ES}$ lowers
mean delay by up to $1.25\times$ ($20\%$) and p99 by up to $1.43\times$
($30\%$) at the arm level, and by up to $1.85\times$ and $2.99\times$ ($46\%$ and $67\%$) on
the best placement with no $\mathrm{CC}$ or $\PL$ overheads. The gain appears under BL on two arms and under DP on the third, which confirms it is a property of the placement, not of the selection.

This lever has no effect on dominant-flow traffic, as expected. For example, on VGG-16 $(32,4,8)$, where one pair carries $92\%$ of the peak link, max$\mathrm{ES}$ returns nothing. This can be predicted by whether the climb can move $\mathrm{ES}$.

\subsection{Routing and Selection}
\label{sec:r3}

The last layer acts at run time. With packing and placement fixed, selection
decides which of the admissible outputs a packet takes, and we compare the
local buffer-level policy (BL) used so far against the distributed cost-to-go
policy (DP) on identical placements and seeds, so that the policy is the only
difference between paired runs.

DP's effect is on the \emph{spread} of delay across placements, the
mapping layer's main risk. DP narrows the spread in all eight populations in
which BL exhibits one --- three workloads, both regimes, $n = 7$--$24$
placements at three seeds, read inside each policy's knee window --- by
$1.09\times$ to $7.50\times$; Fig.~\ref{fig:spread} shows one per regime.
The mechanism is rescue of the worst placements rather than acceleration of
the best: on the fastest placement of each population BL matches or beats
DP, while on the slowest DP improves delay by up to $7.6\times$ ($5\,566$ to
$732$ cycles).

\subsection{Policy Choice and Its Limit}
\label{sec:r4}
We ask which fixed policy is better over everything measured, and how much a
policy that could switch per operating point would add. Every knee-window
placement $\times$ load cell from the mapping and routing populations of
Secs.~\ref{sec:r2}--\ref{sec:r3} is used --- $701$
above the floor, $128$ below, three seeds each --- and three policies are
compared on identical cells: always-BL, always-DP, and a per-cell oracle
taking the better of the two.

DP is ahead of BL in both regimes, on $65$ of $71$ above-floor placements
and in $61$ of the $62$ load bands measured: pooled over cells, by
$1.85\times$ in mean delay and $2.39\times$ in p99 above the floor, and by
$1.15\times$ and $1.23\times$ below it (the blue dotted lines in the routing
panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:layers_p99}). The same
panel resolves this per workload: $1.62$--$3.10\times$
above the floor on all three, $1.15$--$1.26\times$ below it on ResNet-50 and
VGG-16, and level ($1.01\times$) on DeiT-S below the floor --- the arm that
Fig.~\ref{fig:es} accordingly reads under BL. The oracle (the grey lines of
the same panel) adds $1.1\%$
and $2.5\%$ above the floor but $3.8\%$ and $7.7\%$ below it: of the
improvement available between always-BL and the oracle, always-DP captures
$99\%$ and $98\%$ above the floor but only $81\%$ and $76\%$ below it.

The remaining $4$--$8\%$ below the floor is the only headroom left, so we ask
whether the cells DP loses can be identified at design time: no offline
quantity survives a held-out packing, and a pre-registered model over $15$
features of $56$ placements reaches $R^2 = -0.03$ on DP's gain when a packing
is held out, against a registered ceiling of $0.10$.

Taken together in Fig.~\ref{fig:layers_p99}, each layer read against its own
baseline at its own operating point, packing carries the gain
($18.6$--$107\times$), mapping adds $1.26$--$1.43\times$, and selection adds
what the regime allows. Mean delay gives the same picture at smaller
magnitudes: the layer ordering is unchanged, and the oracle sits $1.1\%$ from
DP above the floor and $3.8\%$ below it.
